# Pattern Reference — clarity

Source: adapted from pstack's `unslop` pattern list, restructured around one rule this version doesn't compromise on:

**No pattern here is an absolute ban. Every flag is "consider this," none are "always cut this." Frequency and context decide, not presence.**

pstack's original rule "avoid em dashes entirely" is the example of what this skill must never do — it's mechanical substitution, the exact failure mode this skill exists to avoid. If this skill ever produces a rule shaped like "never use X," that's a bug in the skill, not a valid output.

---

## Tier 1 — near-universal cut candidates

These are close to always worth removing wherever they appear, because they're rarely doing real work — they're filler that sounds like meaning without adding any:

- Significance inflation: "pivotal moment," "testament to," "evolving landscape," "setting the stage for," "indelible mark," "deeply rooted" (unless a character's dialogue in fiction genuinely calls for elevated diction — see domain note below)
- Empty superlatives with nothing under them: "powerful," "seamless," "robust," used as decoration rather than backed by a specific claim
- Meta-commentary about the writing itself: "let's dive into," "it's worth noting that"
- AI-vocabulary tells: "delve," "tapestry," and similar words that show up disproportionately in generated text relative to how people actually talk

Flag these on sight. Still show the reasoning, still let the human confirm — but don't hedge on recommending the cut.

## Tier 2 — frequency-dependent, not presence-dependent

These are legitimate tools that become tells only when overused or used identically every time:

- Em dashes — a tool, not a tell. Flag only when density is high (roughly more than one or two per paragraph, or the same em-dash-as-aside construction repeated back to back). Never recommend removing all of them.
- Colons as connectors — same logic. One well-placed colon is clarity. Five in a row is a pattern.
- Rule-of-three constructions ("clear, concise, and direct") — fine occasionally, a tell when every paragraph reaches for one
- "It's not just X, it's Y" — fine once in a long piece for genuine emphasis, a tell as a recurring structural crutch
- Symmetrical parallelism across sentences — can be intentional rhythm (especially in fiction) or generated-sounding regularity. Judge by whether it's varied elsewhere in the piece or the only rhythm present throughout.

For Tier 2, the output should describe the *pattern*, not flag individual instances: "em dash appears 6 times in 4 paragraphs, worth varying" rather than flagging each one as if it were independently wrong.

## Tier 3 — register-dependent, off by default in fiction

These read as tells in technical/professional writing and can be exactly right in creative writing:

- Elevated or ceremonial diction ("testament to," ornate phrasing)
- Heavy use of "I" and stated opinion — right for a blog post or notebook entry, often wrong for third-person narrative or in-world text
- "Have opinions, react to facts" — this is good advice for a writer's own voice in a notebook or blog post, and the wrong instruction to give an elevated or formal narrative voice in fiction

## Soul-addition guidance (kept from the original, unchanged in spirit)

Removing patterns is half the job — sterile writing with no tells left is still bad writing. Where the piece allows it:
- Vary sentence rhythm — short sentences, then ones that take their time
- Be specific rather than abstract when a concrete detail is available
- Acknowledge complexity rather than flattening to one clean claim
- Let a little unevenness stay — perfectly regular structure reads as generated regardless of vocabulary
